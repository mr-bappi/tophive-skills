# Creative diversity engine — distinct products, distinct design judgment

Load for a **new** website, landing page, greenfield brand concept, showcase, or application whose visual direction is not already locked. This is not permission to rebrand an existing product. The user's approved visual system, target repo design tokens, accessibility, truthful capabilities and practical UX always take precedence.

## 1. Derive direction from specific product evidence

Construct this short internal decision brief from known facts, not invented brand positioning:

| Dimension | Decision to make |
|---|---|
| User and stakes | Who makes the decision, in what setting, and what is at risk? |
| Product job | What concrete task or outcome differentiates it from generic software? |
| Tone | The warranted relationship: precise, welcoming, energetic, playful, reassuring, rigorous, etc. |
| Proof object | What real interface, workflow, data relationship or physical artifact should be shown? |
| Composition | Which hierarchy and layout best communicate that job? |
| Design language | Appropriate palette roles, typography genre, shapes, surfaces, density, illustration and motion |
| What NOT to borrow | A recognizable aesthetic habit from unrelated demos or a current reference not supported by this product |

Be explicit about *evidence vs assumption*. If brand identity exists, reuse it; never manufacture product features to justify a dramatic visual direction. An author's familiar style is not the product's brand.

For new websites, use COMPOSITION-ENGINE.md and ART-DIRECTION-LIBRARY.md **first**, then validate choices here. The external eight-topology COMPOSITION-ATLAS.html is intentionally schematic, not a mandatory design menu.

## 2. Choose a coherent composition, not a preset

Do not select from a fixed menu of templates. Derive design choices as a system:

1. **Narrative & architecture:** Is the buyer deciding based on control, trust, craft, speed, cost, fun, collaboration, or technical detail? Place proof accordingly.
2. **Typographic voice:** Choose scale, line breaks, sans/serif/mono roles and optical spacing for the user, content and available fonts. A bold sans plus italic serif is **one option**, not a default.
3. **Palette & material:** Set colors by brand, environment and contrast; use accent intentionally. Avoid reusing cream + forest green + sage solely because another demo used them.
4. **Geometry:** Frame width, edge behavior, alignment, gutters, grids, corners, strokes, depth and section transitions may change between unrelated brands.
5. **Proof & interaction:** Real visual demonstrations should express this product's specific flow; do not reuse generic three-feature cards, imaginary KPI charts or the same dark-green terminal mock.
6. **Rhythm:** Vary density according to actual content questions; a precise compact screen can be better than a long decorative scroll.

Consider an unconventional direction only when it makes the content clearer, more memorable or more appropriate; being different for its own sake is not craft. Product truth + accessibility > novelty.

## 3. Visual-family collision check

Before implementation, compare proposed direction to any **actually accessible** recent outputs or references, without assuming they share a brand. If no prior outputs are accessible, evaluate genericness rather than invent comparisons.

Ask:

- Does a different product receive essentially the same **headline system, palette, hero split, section order and proof card**?
- Could replacing the logo, title and accent color make the designs interchangeable?
- Are only two tokens changing while layout, hierarchy, silhouette and use of evidence are identical?
- Does an existing design constraint justify genuine similarity? Shared design-system identity, same app ecosystem or approved brand tokens may *require* consistency; do not penalize it.
- Is the new direction actually less legible or harder to implement due to novelty?

If the page is interchangeable without a valid shared brand reason, revise at least **two structural axes** (composition / narrative / proof / typography / interaction), not just color, while keeping product facts and functional constraints intact.

## 4. Avoid style overfitting

The following are design choices, not skill defaults: off-white background; deep evergreen; muted sage; giant left headline and right product card; italic serif emphasis; uppercase mono eyebrows; thin editorial rulers; full-width dark-green interlude; a succession of 01/02/03 numbered workflow cards; lime accents; tilted sticky notes. They may fit a product, but must be justified per brief.

Do not pick fonts, palette, illustrations, layouts or imagery by randomization, arbitrary category stereotyping, or an unrelated example. If a developer tool genuinely benefits from a terminal, use it; a collaboration app may need a board, a booking dashboard needs real operations instead of marketing.

## 5. Record intentional differences (internally)

Before coding, keep a compact direction note:
- **Why this direction:** one sentence tied to user/product evidence.
- **Signature:** one product-specific visual and/or interaction mechanism.
- **Not a clone of:** an identifiable pattern that would not fit this brief.
- **Risk and mitigation:** dense data, mobile, contrast, performance, reduced motion, truthful proof.

Do not display a long rationale to users during a direct BUILD task. A user receives the implementation first; summarize choices and tests on handoff.

## 6. Dashboard exception

For operational admin apps, first maintain user-task priority, navigation, data clarity, state density and interactions. A brandable UI still needs an identity, but it should not force stylized hero compositions, huge typography or novelty that makes forms and tables harder to use. See `DASHBOARD-WORKFLOW.md`.
